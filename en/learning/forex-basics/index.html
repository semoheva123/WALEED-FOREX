Forex basics: how are currency pairs and prices read?

Foreign exchange (forex or FX) concerns the value of one currency relative to another. Before assessing a platform or decision, understand how pairs are quoted, what execution costs mean, and why knowledge alone cannot determine financial outcomes.

1. What is the foreign exchange market?

Forex is a market in which one currency is valued against another. Participants have different purposes, including payments, hedging and managing currency exposure; some also seek to benefit from price changes. Activity spans international centres, and the retail customer’s product and execution model may differ from the inter-dealer market.

Many retail products are contracts for difference (CFDs) or other derivatives; they do not necessarily deliver the currencies to the customer. Rights, costs and execution depend on the contract, legal entity and residence. Read the product documents rather than assume all forex trading works the same way.

2. How is a currency pair read?

In EUR/USD, EUR is the base currency and USD is the quote currency. A quote of 1.0800 means one unit of the base currency is quoted at 1.0800 units of the quote currency. Buying the pair represents exposure to the base currency relative to the quote currency; it does not mean the underlying currencies are delivered in every product.

Pairs are often grouped as major, minor or exotic, though classifications vary. Liquidity, spreads and availability may depend on the pair, time and provider. A “major” label does not guarantee low costs.

3. Bid, ask and the pip

The ask is the price at which a customer may buy and the bid the price at which a customer may sell, subject to the provider’s terms. The difference is the spread, which can change. A pip is a common unit for measuring price movement; for many currency pairs it is 0.0001, while yen pairs and some platforms use different conventions. Check the instrument specification and account currency.

A displayed quote is not a guarantee of execution. Volatility, liquidity, slippage, technical interruptions and order type can affect execution. Review the execution policy, how spreads are measured and the quote-data provider.

4. Which costs should be considered?

  • Spread: the bid–ask difference; it may widen at certain times.
  • Commission: it may be charged per trade or volume, depending on the account.
  • Overnight financing (swap): a charge or credit may apply when a position is held past a rollover time.
  • Funding and conversion: deposit, withdrawal or third-party currency conversion charges may apply.
  • Execution costs: slippage or requotes may affect the final outcome.

Do not compare accounts using a quoted spread alone. Compare total cost for the same instrument, volume and time, and consult the current fee schedule and contract.

5. Understand the limits of information

Margin trading magnifies exposure, so losses may exceed what a small price movement might suggest. Past results and illustrations do not predict future performance. Do not use money needed for essential expenses. A demo account may not fully reproduce slippage, emotions or live execution conditions.
